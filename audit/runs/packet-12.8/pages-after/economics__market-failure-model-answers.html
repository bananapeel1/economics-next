Define the term AO1

A negative externality is a spill-over cost imposed on third parties who are not involved in the transaction between producer and consumer. It is therefore not reflected in the market price.

Earns 1 mark · 1–2 marks — definition The third-party clause is the first definition mark. "A cost to society" does not earn it — society includes the two parties who chose to trade.
Earns 1 mark · 1–2 marks — definition The second definition mark. Without this clause the definition tops out at one, because nothing yet says why the market gets the quantity wrong.

Develop one example AO2

For example, a coal-fired power station emits carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide as by-products of electricity production. Local residents and future generations suffer increased rates of respiratory illness and climate-related damage — costs they bear without compensation. Because these social costs are external to the firm’s calculations, the market over-produces electricity relative to the socially optimal output.

Earns 1 mark · 3–4 marks — developed example A named example AND the harm it does to named third parties. "Pollution affects people" supplies neither and stays in the 1–2 band.
Earns 1 mark · 3–4 marks — developed example This is the clause the 3–4 band calls "developed": the example is carried through to over-production rather than left as an illustration.
Examiner’s note
Full marks require both a precise definition (spill-over cost, third parties, not in price) and a developed example that identifies the specific third-party harm. Vague answers like "pollution affects people" will only reach 2 marks. Linking over-production to the divergence between private and social cost pushes into top-band application.

What the diagram shows

The vertical axis is price, cost and benefit; the horizontal axis is the quantity of vaccinations. Marginal private benefit (MPB) slopes down and marginal private cost, which equals marginal social cost (MPC = MSC), slopes up; the free market settles where they cross, at quantity Q1. Marginal social benefit (MSB) lies above MPB: the vertical gap is the external benefit to people protected by someone else’s vaccination, such as babies too young to be vaccinated. The social optimum is where MSB = MSC, at Q*, to the right of Q1: left to the free market, too few vaccinations are bought. The welfare loss is the triangle between Q1 and Q*, bounded above by MSB and below by MSC: the vaccinations whose social benefit exceeds their cost but which the market does not provide.

Earns 1 mark · Knowledge (1 mark) The knowledge mark: the market as the family that buys a vaccination sees it. MPC = MSC because nothing in the context says producing the vaccine harms anyone.
Earns 1 mark · Application (3 marks) The context’s external benefit, drawn. MSB above MPB is what makes this a positive externality of consumption.
Earns 1 mark · Application (3 marks) Both quantities marked, in the right order. Q* to the right of Q1 is the under-consumption the question is about.
Earns 1 mark · Application (3 marks) The mark the question names. Only the triangle between the two quantities is lost; the area to the left of Q1 is benefit the market does deliver.
Examiner’s note
All four marks are on the diagram; the sentences under it say what each mark is for and are not needed in the exam. The commonest loss is drawing MSB below MPB — that is the diagram for an external cost of consumption, and it puts the welfare loss on the wrong side of the market quantity. The next is shading the whole area between the curves instead of the triangle between the market quantity and the social optimum.

The two kinds of good AO1

A public good is non-rival — one person’s use does not reduce what is left for others — and non-excludable — people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting. A private good is the opposite: rival and excludable.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) The first knowledge mark: both characteristics, each with what it means.
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) The second knowledge mark. The question asks why the sandbags are NOT a public good, so the contrast is part of the answer.

The context AO2

The sea wall is both: protecting one more house behind it takes no protection away from the houses next to it, and a household that refuses to pay cannot be left unprotected while the houses on either side are kept dry. A sandbag is rival — a bag piled against one door cannot also protect the house next door — and excludable, because the shop sells it only to people who pay for it, so it is a private good.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: both characteristics shown on the wall, in terms of the houses behind it.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: the same two tests applied to the sandbags, with the opposite result.
Examiner’s note
Full marks: both characteristics are defined, and each is then shown on the context rather than restated. "The sea wall benefits everyone" earns nothing on its own — the marks are for saying why protecting one more house costs its neighbours nothing, and why a household that does not pay cannot be left out.

Model answer

Marked 4 / 4 · Select a sentence to see why it scores

The terms AO1

Asymmetric information exists when one party to a transaction knows more than the other; here the pension provider knows far more about its fees, risks and likely returns than the saver does. Resources are misallocated when they go to uses that people would not choose if they were fully informed.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) The first knowledge mark, with the direction of the gap stated: the seller knows more.
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) The second knowledge mark: the yardstick the rest of the answer is measured against.

The pensions market AO2

Because the fees are set out in long documents and performance is revealed only after decades, a saver cannot compare funds properly and may choose one with high fees and poor returns, or one that takes more risk than they realise. Savings then flow to funds that a fully informed saver would avoid, and some workers, unsure whom to trust, save too little for retirement — so too few resources go into pensions overall, and too many into poorly run funds.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: both details in the context used to explain why the saver cannot judge.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: the misallocation itself, stated for this market. Without it the answer explains the information gap but not what the question asks it to lead to.
Examiner’s note
Full marks. The definition names who knows more — the provider — rather than saying information is "imperfect", and the application uses both details in the context: fees in long documents, and results revealed only after decades. The last clause is what the question asks for and many answers stop short of: where resources actually go when savers cannot tell good funds from bad.

The two costs AO1

The private cost of consuming a good is the cost borne by the consumer who chooses to consume it: for a smoker, the price of the pack and the harm to their own health. The social cost is the private cost plus the external cost, the cost that falls on third parties who did not choose to consume, so social cost is greater than private cost by exactly the external cost.

The externality and the charge AO1

A negative externality of consumption occurs when consuming a good imposes a cost on third parties, so its marginal social benefit (MSB) lies below its marginal private benefit (MPB), leading to over-consumption and allocative inefficiency at the free-market equilibrium. The per bag charge acts as an indirect (Pigouvian) tax on the consumer, designed to internalise the estimated external cost per bag.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) Knowledge: the gap between private and social benefit, and the over-consumption it causes. MSB below MPB is how the diagram the data-response page names (MPB, MSB, MPC = MSC) shows an external cost of consumption. It is the consumer’s act that causes the cost here — a factory example would answer a different question.
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) Knowledge: what the charge is and what it is for. The charge is above the estimated external cost; whether that over-corrects is evaluation, which Appendix 6 says Analyse does not include, so the answer rightly leaves it alone.

The extract’s figures, interpreted AO2

Applied to the UAE case, because the stimulus gives a price elasticity of demand of for single-use plastic bags, demand is price elastic. The 0.25 AED charge therefore causes a more-than-proportionate fall in quantity demanded. This is consistent with the roughly reduction in consumption reported by Dubai Municipality in the first year.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: the figure interpreted, not just quoted. "Elastic, so more than proportionate" is the interpretation the mark is for.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: the second figure, tied to the first. The 45% fall is what an elastic response looks like; quoted without that link it is a number, not evidence.

The chain of reasoning AO3

The chain of reasoning is: the charge raises the private cost of consumption closer to the full social cost, which shifts consumer behaviour along the demand curve, which reduces the quantity consumed towards the socially optimal level, which in turn reduces the welfare loss triangle associated with over-consumption. The result is an improvement in allocative efficiency in the Dubai retail market.

Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (2 marks) Analysis: the first chain, from the charge to fewer bags, each step causing the next.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (2 marks) Analysis: the chain carried to where the question points — the social optimum and a smaller welfare loss. An answer that stops at "fewer bags" has not said how the market failure is corrected.
Examiner’s note
Full marks, because the extract’s figures are interpreted rather than quoted: the elasticity of -1.4 is linked explicitly to the size of the behavioural response, and the 45% fall is used as the evidence of it. An answer that explains the theory of Pigouvian taxes but stays generic and quotes no data misses both application marks. Analyse does not reward evaluation, so weighing whether an AED 0.25 charge over-corrects an AED 0.18 external cost would earn nothing here — it belongs in a longer question.

Where the optimum is AO1

The socially optimal level of consumption is where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost; with a negative externality of consumption the free market goes past it, because shoppers ignore the cost their bags impose on others. A charge brings consumption to the optimum only if it equals the marginal external cost there: it then adds the value of the damage each bag does to its price, and no more.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) Knowledge: the optimum defined by the marginal condition, not as "the best amount".
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks) Knowledge: the rule the rest of the answer tests. Without it, 0.25 against 0.18 is two numbers, not an argument.

The charge against the damage AO2 · AO3

Table 1 estimates the external cost at per bag, but the charge is — about 40% more than the estimated damage. If the estimate is right, the charge over-corrects: a shopper now refuses any bag worth less than to them, including bags worth between and , whose benefit was greater than their full social cost. Consumption is pushed below the social optimum, and a welfare loss from under-consumption replaces part of the loss from over-consumption.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: the table’s figure set against the extract’s charge, with the gap sized (0.25 ÷ 0.18 ≈ 1.39).
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (2 marks) Analysis: the chain from the overshoot to a new welfare loss. It is the step part (c) did not need, because (c) asked how the charge corrects the failure, not whether it lands on the optimum.

How far past the optimum AO2 · AO3

Because demand is price elastic ( ), the extra AED 0.07 on each bag moves consumption further past the optimum than it would if demand were inelastic, so the overshoot is larger in bags, not only in dirhams. The scale is large: a fall on the extract’s estimate of around bags a year is roughly 5 billion fewer bags, so even a small overshoot per bag is spread across billions of them.

Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (2 marks) Analysis: the elasticity used to size the overshoot, a different job from part (c), where it explained the size of the fall.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks) Application: two figures from the text combined into one the extract does not print (0.45 × 11 billion ≈ 5 billion).

Introduction AO1

Moral hazard occurs when an economic agent takes greater risks because it knows that some of the cost of things going wrong will be borne by someone else. Deposit protection is a promise, usually by a government-backed insurer, that savers will get their money back, up to a limit, if their bank fails. Banking already suffers from asymmetric information — depositors cannot see how their bank lends their money — so the question is whether protection makes the resulting risk-taking worse than the failures it prevents.

The case that protection does harm AO2 · AO3

Protection weakens the discipline depositors would otherwise impose. A saver whose deposit is guaranteed has no reason to check how risky the bank’s lending is, or to move to a safer bank, so a risky bank can keep attracting deposits without paying a higher interest rate for them. Its managers and shareholders then keep the profits if risky loans pay off, while losses beyond their own capital fall on the insurer, and possibly on taxpayers, if they do not. The bank takes more risk than it would if it bore the whole cost. Credit flows to riskier projects than is efficient — a misallocation of resources, which is what makes moral hazard a market failure rather than only a risk to the bank. The 2023 case strengthens the argument. The US authorities protected all deposits at the two failed banks, including balances above the USD 250,000 limit that deposit insurance normally covers. Much of those banks’ funding came from large deposits held by firms — exactly the depositors best placed to monitor a bank — so guaranteeing them after the event signals that even uninsured deposits may be rescued, and future large depositors have less reason to check.

Define the externality AO1

A negative externality of production occurs when the production of a good imposes costs on third parties who are not involved in the transaction. The marginal social cost (MSC) exceeds the marginal private cost (MPC) — the gap between them is the external cost.

Earns 1 mark · Level 1 — knowledge (1–2 marks) Level 1 knowledge. The third-party clause is the mark; "production harms the environment" is not.
Earns 1 mark · Level 1 — knowledge (1–2 marks) The second Level 1 mark. Naming the gap is what lets every later step be about a quantity rather than a feeling.

Apply it to a context AO2 · AO3

For example, a steel factory discharging chemical waste into a nearby river imposes costs on downstream fisheries, local residents, and the environment. The factory only considers its private costs (raw materials, energy, wages) when deciding how much to produce. It does not pay for the pollution damage — this external cost falls on third parties.

Earns 1 mark · Level 2 — applied to the context (3–4 marks) Level 2 application: a named producer and named third parties. An unnamed "firm" leaves the answer in Level 1.
Earns 1 mark · Level 2 — applied to the context (3–4 marks) Begins the chain: the decision rule, not just the harm. This is where Level 2 turns into the start of Level 3.

Carry the chain to the welfare loss AO3

Because the market price reflects only private costs, not social costs, the price is too low and output is too high relative to the socially optimal level. On a diagram, the free market produces at Q₁ (where MPC = MPB) but the social optimum is at Q* (where MSC = MSB). The area between Q* and Q₁, bounded by MSC and MSB, is the deadweight welfare loss — the excess cost to society from overproduction.

Earns 1 mark · Level 3 — developed chain in context (5–6 marks) Level 3: the chain is carried through to a misallocation, in that order. Stating "the market fails" without this step is Level 2.
Earns 1 mark · Level 3 — developed chain in context (5–6 marks) The diagram credit the Level 3 descriptor asks for. The triangle has to be identified, not just drawn.

Name the failure — and where the assessment should have gone AO3 · AO4

This is a clear case of market failure because the price mechanism sends the wrong signal — it tells producers to produce more than is socially desirable. Resources are misallocated because the market does not account for the full cost of production. Government intervention (e.g. a Pigouvian tax equal to the external cost) could internalise the externality and move output toward the social optimum.

Examiner’s note
The chain is developed and correct: external cost → MSC above MPC → price too low → overproduction → deadweight welfare loss, with the steel factory as application and the welfare loss triangle available for the diagram credit. It sits at Level 3 because the assessment is implied rather than made — the Pigouvian tax in the final paragraph is offered as a remedy, not weighed. A Level 4 needs a brief assessment: how large the misallocation is, and whether intervention improves on it, depends on whether the external cost can actually be valued, and pollution damage rarely can be valued precisely.
