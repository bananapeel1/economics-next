Dubai expands single-use plastics ban as Gulf sugar tax debate returns

The UAE's federal single-use plastics policy has moved into its next phase in 2025, with restaurants, delivery platforms and supermarkets across Dubai and Abu Dhabi now required to charge a minimum of AED 0.25 per plastic bag and eliminate single-use plastic cups, stirrers and cutlery from their operations. Officials estimate that around 11 billion single-use plastic bags were used annually across the country before the policy began tightening in 2024, and early data from Dubai Municipality suggests consumption fell by approximately 45% in the first year of the charge.

At the same time, a renewed policy debate has emerged in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) around excise taxes on sugary drinks. The UAE and Saudi Arabia already apply a 50% excise on soft drinks and 100% on energy drinks, introduced in 2017. A 2024 Ministry of Health review reported that per capita consumption of carbonated soft drinks in the UAE declined by roughly 32% over the seven years following introduction, though diabetes prevalence remains high at an estimated 12.3% of the adult population. Analysts at regional think-tank Gulf Economic Review argue that the current rate is now under-correcting, given that private healthcare costs linked to obesity and type-2 diabetes in the GCC are estimated at over USD 25 billion per year.

Critics, including several food and beverage industry groups in the region, argue that the taxes are regressive, hitting lower-income expatriate workers hardest, and that producers have largely passed the cost through to consumers without significant reformulation of products.

    Explain what is meant by a negative externality and give one example.

    Attempt it, then mark it

    0 of 4 marks claimed — 0 of 4 criteria ticked

    • 1–2 marks — definition

    • 3–4 marks — developed example

    The marked script — where each of those marks is earned

    Define the termAO1

    A negative externality is a spill-over cost imposed on third parties who are not involved in the transaction between producer and consumer.

    The third-party clause is the first definition mark. "A cost to society" does not earn it — society includes the two parties who chose to trade.

    It is therefore not reflected in the market price.

    The second definition mark. Without this clause the definition tops out at one, because nothing yet says why the market gets the quantity wrong.

    Develop one exampleAO2

    For example, a coal-fired power station emits carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide as by-products of electricity production. Local residents and future generations suffer increased rates of respiratory illness and climate-related damage — costs they bear without compensation.

    A named example AND the harm it does to named third parties. "Pollution affects people" supplies neither and stays in the 1–2 band.

    Because these social costs are external to the firm’s calculations, the market over-produces electricity relative to the socially optimal output.

    This is the clause the 3–4 band calls "developed": the example is carried through to over-production rather than left as an illustration.

    Mark scheme
    1–2 marks
    Definition of negative externality (spill-over cost to third parties not reflected in price)
    3–4 marks
    Clear, developed example with reference to third-party cost and market over-production

    Full marks require both a precise definition (spill-over cost, third parties, not in price) and a developed example that identifies the specific third-party harm. Vague answers like "pollution affects people" will only reach 2 marks. Linking over-production to the divergence between private and social cost pushes into top-band application.

  2. Examine8 marksAO1 · AO2 · AO3 · AO411 min

    Examine how a negative externality of production leads to market failure.

    Attempt it, then mark it

    Saved in this browser only, against this question. Close the tab and come back and it is still here; open it on another device and it is not. Nothing is sent anywhere.

    0 of 8 marks claimed — 0 of 8 criteria ticked

    • Level 1 — knowledge (1–2 marks)

    • Level 2 — applied to the context (3–4 marks)

    • Level 3 — developed chain in context (5–6 marks)

    • Level 4 — brief assessment (7–8 marks)

    The marked script — where each of those marks is earned

    Define the externalityAO1

    A negative externality of production occurs when the production of a good imposes costs on third parties who are not involved in the transaction.

    Level 1 knowledge. The third-party clause is the mark; "production harms the environment" is not.

    The marginal social cost (MSC) exceeds the marginal private cost (MPC) — the gap between them is the external cost.

    The second Level 1 mark. Naming the gap is what lets every later step be about a quantity rather than a feeling.

    Apply it to a contextAO2 · AO3

    For example, a steel factory discharging chemical waste into a nearby river imposes costs on downstream fisheries, local residents, and the environment.

    Level 2 application: a named producer and named third parties. An unnamed "firm" leaves the answer in Level 1.

    The factory only considers its private costs (raw materials, energy, wages) when deciding how much to produce. It does not pay for the pollution damage — this external cost falls on third parties.

    Begins the chain: the decision rule, not just the harm. This is where Level 2 turns into the start of Level 3.

    Carry the chain to the welfare lossAO3

    Because the market price reflects only private costs, not social costs, the price is too low and output is too high relative to the socially optimal level.

    Level 3: the chain is carried through to a misallocation, in that order. Stating "the market fails" without this step is Level 2.

    On a diagram, the free market produces at Q₁ (where MPC = MPB) but the social optimum is at Q* (where MSC = MSB). The area between Q* and Q₁, bounded by MSC and MSB, is the deadweight welfare loss — the excess cost to society from overproduction.

    The diagram credit the Level 3 descriptor asks for. The triangle has to be identified, not just drawn.

    Name the failure — and where the assessment should have goneAO3 · AO4

    This is a clear case of market failure because the price mechanism sends the wrong signal — it tells producers to produce more than is socially desirable. Resources are misallocated because the market does not account for the full cost of production.

    THIS SCRIPT STOPS HERE and asserts the misallocation. Level 4 wants it weighed: how large is it? The answer never says, which is why the commentary puts this script at 5–6.

    Government intervention (e.g. a Pigouvian tax equal to the external cost) could internalise the externality and move output toward the social optimum.

    A remedy offered, not weighed — the exact clause the examiner commentary names as the difference between Level 3 and Level 4. Weighing it means asking whether pollution damage can be valued precisely enough to set the tax, and it rarely can.

    Evaluate the view that government intervention is always necessary to correct market failure.

    The marked script — where each of those marks is earned

    IntroductionAO1 · AO3

    Market failure occurs when the free market leads to a misallocation of resources — producing too much (negative externalities), too little (positive externalities, merit goods), or none at all (public goods).

    The definition mark. The three-way split is what lets the essay pick its cases later instead of arguing about one.

    The question is whether government intervention is always required to correct these failures, or whether alternative mechanisms — including private bargaining and market-based solutions — can sometimes achieve efficient outcomes without state action.

    Reading "always" is the whole essay. An answer that argues intervention is useful has answered a question that was not asked.

    Argument 1 — intervention is necessaryAO1 · AO2 · AO3

    In many cases, government intervention is essential because the market cannot self-correct.

    The claim the paragraph then has to earn. On its own it is worth nothing.

    Public goods such as street lighting and national defence are non-excludable and non-rivalrous — the free rider problem means no private firm can profitably supply them. Only the state can fund provision through taxation.

    Knowledge, and the strongest case for "always": with a pure public good there is no private route at all.

    The UK’s sugar tax (Soft Drinks Industry Levy), introduced in 2018, reduced sugary drink consumption by 34% and incentivised manufacturers to reformulate products.

    Two application marks, because it is a named policy with a measured effect and a second-order effect. An example with no number earns one at most.

    Without this intervention, the overconsumption of sugar — and its associated health-system costs — would have continued uncorrected.

    The counterfactual. It is what turns an example into an argument, and most scripts leave it out.

    Argument 2 — intervention is not always necessaryAO1 · AO2 · AO3

    The Coase theorem suggests that if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are low, private parties can negotiate an efficient outcome without government involvement.

    Knowledge, and the two conditions matter: quoting Coase without them makes the counter-argument unfalsifiable.

    For example, if a factory pollutes a farmer’s land and the farmer has clear legal ownership, they can negotiate compensation or a reduction in pollution — reaching the socially optimal output through bargaining.

    Application to a case where the two conditions actually hold: two parties, one clear title.

    In practice, however, transaction costs are often high and property rights unclear, limiting the applicability of this approach.

    Analysis that turns the counter-argument into a bounded one. Without it the essay argues against itself in the conclusion.

    Evaluation — government failureAO1 · AO3 · AO4

    Even when intervention is attempted, it can lead to government failure — where the intervention makes the allocation of resources worse rather than better.

    "Worse rather than better" is the definition mark. Government failure is not intervention that merely disappoints.

    Governments face information failure — they may not know the exact size of an external cost, leading them to set taxes too high or too low.

    The chain that connects back to the first argument: the same tax praised above can miss in either direction.

    Regulatory capture means regulators may serve industry interests rather than the public.

    Evaluation, because it questions whose interest the intervention serves — a different objection from "it did not work".

    Unintended consequences are common — for example, minimum pricing on alcohol may harm low-income moderate drinkers without significantly reducing problem drinking.

    Application inside the evaluation, and it names who is harmed. A generic "there may be side effects" earns nothing.

    The cost of intervention itself (bureaucracy, enforcement, compliance) may exceed the welfare gain from correcting the failure.

    The comparison the conclusion will turn on, stated once here so the conclusion introduces no new material.

    ConclusionAO4

    On balance, government intervention is often necessary but not always effective or desirable. For pure public goods and large-scale externalities where private solutions are impractical, intervention is essential.

    Two marks: a conditional verdict that answers "always" with "not always, and here is when". A yes or a no cannot reach the top band on this question.

    However, the form and extent of intervention matters — well-designed market-based instruments (tradable permits, targeted taxes) tend to outperform heavy-handed regulation.

    Judging the form, not just the fact. It is the move that separates a top-band conclusion from a summary.

    The key judgement is whether the costs of government failure are likely to be greater or less than the costs of market failure left uncorrected. Where transaction costs are low and property rights clear, private solutions should be tried first.

    Two marks. This is the evaluative framework itself — a criterion for deciding, not another opinion — and it closes the loop on both arguments.
