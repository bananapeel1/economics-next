The externality and the chargeAO1

A negative externality of consumption occurs when the marginal social cost (MSC) of a good exceeds its marginal private cost (MPC), leading to over-consumption and allocative inefficiency at the free-market equilibrium. The per bag charge acts as an indirect (Pigouvian) tax on the consumer, designed to internalise the estimated external cost per bag.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (2 marks)Knowledge: what the charge is and what it is for. The charge is above the estimated external cost; whether that over-corrects is evaluation, which Appendix 6 says Analyse does not include, so the answer rightly leaves it alone.

The extract’s figures, interpretedAO2

This is consistent with the roughly reduction in consumption reported by Dubai Municipality in the first year.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (2 marks)Application: the figure interpreted, not just quoted. "Elastic, so more than proportionate" is the interpretation the mark is for.

The chain of reasoningAO3

Marked 20 / 20 · Select a sentence to see why it scores

IntroductionAO1

A negative externality of consumption is a cost that falls on third parties when a good is consumed and that is not reflected in its market price, so the social cost of consuming the good is greater than the private cost the consumer pays. Sugary drinks carry such a cost — Table 1 estimates it at per litre of carbonated soft drink — alongside an information failure: consumers under-value the long-run health costs they will bear themselves. An excise is an indirect tax that raises the price the consumer pays. The question is not whether the excise works at all but whether it is the most effective way to correct the failure, so it has to be weighed against the alternatives as well as against doing nothing.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)Knowledge: the definition, with both clauses — who bears the cost and why the price misses it. It sets up the whole essay: a tax is only corrective if there is a cost the price leaves out.
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)Knowledge: two sources of market failure, not one. The distinction pays off in the third paragraph, where it decides what a tax can and cannot correct.
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)Knowledge, and a reading of the question. "Most effective" is a comparison; an essay that only asks whether the tax works has answered an easier question than the one set.

The case for the taxAO1 · AO2 · AO3

There is evidence that the tax has worked. Table 1 gives carbonated soft drinks a price elasticity of demand of , so a price rise of up to predicts a fall in quantity demanded of up to about 30% (0.6 × ). The stimulus reports a fall in per capita consumption of roughly over the seven years after 2017 — close to what the elasticity predicts, and consistent with the critics’ claim that producers have largely passed the tax through to consumers. The chain is: the excise raises the price the consumer pays, so the private cost of each drink moves closer to its full social cost; consumers move along their demand curve and buy fewer drinks; and consumption moves from the free-market quantity towards the social optimum, shrinking the welfare loss that over-consumption creates. On a diagram, marginal social benefit lies below marginal private benefit by the external cost; the tax shifts supply upwards, and the closer the tax is to per litre, the closer the new equilibrium lies to the quantity where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (4 marks)Application: the elasticity used to make a prediction, which turns the next figure into a test rather than a quotation.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (4 marks)Application: the 32% set against the prediction. Linking it to pass-through uses a second part of the extract to explain the first.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis: a multi-stage chain, each step causing the next, ending at the welfare loss rather than at "people drink less".
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)Knowledge of the corrective-tax rule, on the diagram the data-response page names (MPB, MSB, MPC = MSC). It also sets up the government-failure paragraph: the rule needs the external cost to be known.

Evaluation — inelastic demand and the health evidenceAO2 · AO3 · AO4

However, a PED of also means demand is inelastic: the tax changes behaviour less than proportionately, and around two-thirds of the pre-tax volume is still being drunk. Gulf Economic Review argues that the rate is under-correcting, pointing to over a year in healthcare costs linked to obesity and type-2 diabetes and to adult diabetes prevalence of . That evidence is weaker than it looks. Diabetes prevalence is a stock built up over decades, so seven years of lower consumption would not be expected to move it much yet; obesity and diabetes have many causes besides soft drinks, so not all of the can be laid at the door of the product being taxed; and the part of that bill paid by drinkers themselves is an internal cost, not an externality — only the part passed to third parties, through pooled insurance premiums for example, justifies a corrective tax. The stronger form of the under-correction argument is therefore about information failure rather than the size of the health bill: a tax set equal to the external cost corrects the externality, but it does nothing about consumers under-valuing the harm to themselves, and with demand this inelastic a price signal alone moves consumption only a little towards what a fully informed consumer would choose.

Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis: the same figure read the other way. Using one number on both sides of the argument is what Appendix 6 means by considering the validity and significance of an argument.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (4 marks)Application: the extract’s case against the tax, stated fairly before it is tested.
Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation: a critical assessment of the evidence, on three separate grounds. The figure is not dismissed, it is sized — the move that separates weighing from listing.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis: the two failures from the introduction pulled apart. It explains why a correctly set tax can still leave the outcome short, which is the argument the think-tank’s figures were reaching for.

Evaluation — the tax is regressiveAO2 · AO3 · AO4

Second, the tax is regressive, and the extract’s own figures show why. If the is passed through in full, as the critics say it largely has been, and consumption is about lower, spending on soft drinks is roughly 1.5 × 0.68 ≈ 1.02 times its pre-tax level: consumers are paying about the same total as before for about two-thirds of the drinks. That is the arithmetic of inelastic demand: when PED is below 1 in absolute value, a price rise increases what consumers spend on the good. The same cash sum is a far larger share of a lower-income expatriate worker’s income than of a high earner’s, so the burden falls hardest on the group the critics name. This matters for the judgement: the inelastic demand that limits the fall in consumption is the same thing that makes the tax cost low-income drinkers more, so the weaker the behavioural effect, the heavier the regressive burden. The burden could be offset if the revenue funded healthcare or transfers for those workers, but the extract gives no evidence that it does.

Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (4 marks)Application: regressivity quantified from the extract rather than asserted. The arithmetic uses only the 50% rate and the 32% fall, and says "roughly" because the 32% is spread over seven years.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis: why the arithmetic makes the tax regressive, carried through to the people the extract names.
Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation: the equity–efficiency trade-off weighed, not named. The two weaknesses are shown to have one cause, and the offset is made conditional on something the extract does not show.

Evaluation — the alternativesAO3 · AO4

Third, the tax may not be the most effective instrument because of what it is levied on. The stimulus reports that producers passed the cost through without significant reformulation. A flat excise is charged on the price of a drink, not on its sugar content, so a producer that halves the sugar in a drink pays the same tax as before and has no reason to do it. A tax charged per gram of sugar, or tiered by sugar content as in the UK’s Soft Drinks Industry Levy, would give producers that reason, and a reformulated drink cuts sugar intake without raising the price consumers pay — which also avoids the regressive burden above. Information provision, such as clear sugar labelling and public health education, tackles the information failure directly, but it works slowly and on its own is unlikely to shift consumption as much as a price signal has. Weighed together, the alternatives are complements that repair the tax’s two weaknesses, not replacements for it.

Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis: the extract’s "without significant reformulation" explained by the design of the tax, not just reported.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis: the alternative carried through its own chain, and tied back to the weakness in the paragraph before.
Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation: the alternatives weighed against the tax and against each other, ending in a verdict on them rather than a list.

Evaluation — government failureAO4

Finally, setting the correct rate is itself a source of government failure. The rate that corrects the externality equals the marginal external cost, per litre, but a excise is a percentage of the price: it equals only if a litre sells for AED 2.40 before tax, and the extract gives no price, so whether the current rate under- or over-corrects cannot be read from the data. The figure is itself a composite estimate, and a percentage tax charges more on a premium brand than on a cheap one with the same sugar content. Set above the external cost, the tax creates a welfare loss of its own through under-consumption; set below it, the market failure persists.

Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation: government failure made specific to this tax. It also answers the think-tank directly — "under-correcting" cannot be shown from the rate, only argued from other evidence.

ConclusionAO4

Overall, the excise has cut consumption by about as much as its elasticity predicts, so it is doing part of its job, but it is unlikely to be the most effective way to correct the market failure on its own. Its weaknesses follow from the elasticity and from what it is levied on: it leaves most consumption in place, it raises what low-income drinkers spend, and, because it is charged on price rather than sugar, it gives producers no reason to reformulate. The judgement therefore turns on design rather than on the tax itself: a sugar-based levy, with the revenue used to offset the burden on lower-income workers and backed by labelling and education, would correct more of the failure at a lower cost in equity than the current flat rate. The tax is necessary but not sufficient, and calling it the most effective way would only hold if long-run demand became considerably more elastic than , so that the price signal alone could carry more of the correction.

Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation: a judgement on the question as set — "most effective" — drawn from the paragraphs above. Nothing new is introduced here.
Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation: the criterion the judgement rests on, and the condition that would reverse it. A conditional verdict of this kind is what Appendix 6 means by an informed judgement.
Examiner’s note
Full marks, because every evaluative point is weighed rather than listed and the judgement follows from the argument before it. The health figures (USD 25 bn, 12.3%) are tested before they are relied on — time lag, attribution, and how much of the bill is external at all — and the regressivity point is quantified from the extract rather than asserted: a 50% price rise on about two-thirds of the volume leaves spending roughly where it was. The conclusion states the criterion it rests on (the -0.6 elasticity, and a tax levied on price rather than sugar) and the condition that would reverse it. Every figure is taken or derived from the extract; none is invented.

Define the externalityAO1

A negative externality of production occurs when the production of a good imposes costs on third parties who are not involved in the transaction. The marginal social cost (MSC) exceeds the marginal private cost (MPC) — the gap between them is the external cost.

Earns 1 mark · Level 1 — knowledge (1–2 marks)Level 1 knowledge. The third-party clause is the mark; "production harms the environment" is not.
Earns 1 mark · Level 1 — knowledge (1–2 marks)The second Level 1 mark. Naming the gap is what lets every later step be about a quantity rather than a feeling.

Apply it to a contextAO2 · AO3

For example, a steel factory discharging chemical waste into a nearby river imposes costs on downstream fisheries, local residents, and the environment. The factory only considers its private costs (raw materials, energy, wages) when deciding how much to produce. It does not pay for the pollution damage — this external cost falls on third parties.

Earns 1 mark · Level 2 — applied to the context (3–4 marks)Level 2 application: a named producer and named third parties. An unnamed "firm" leaves the answer in Level 1.
Earns 1 mark · Level 2 — applied to the context (3–4 marks)Begins the chain: the decision rule, not just the harm. This is where Level 2 turns into the start of Level 3.

Carry the chain to the welfare lossAO3

Because the market price reflects only private costs, not social costs, the price is too low and output is too high relative to the socially optimal level. On a diagram, the free market produces at Q₁ (where MPC = MPB) but the social optimum is at Q* (where MSC = MSB). The area between Q* and Q₁, bounded by MSC and MSB, is the deadweight welfare loss — the excess cost to society from overproduction.

Earns 1 mark · Level 3 — developed chain in context (5–6 marks)Level 3: the chain is carried through to a misallocation, in that order. Stating "the market fails" without this step is Level 2.
Earns 1 mark · Level 3 — developed chain in context (5–6 marks)The diagram credit the Level 3 descriptor asks for. The triangle has to be identified, not just drawn.

Name the failure — and where the assessment should have goneAO3 · AO4

This is a clear case of market failure because the price mechanism sends the wrong signal — it tells producers to produce more than is socially desirable. Resources are misallocated because the market does not account for the full cost of production. Government intervention (e.g. a Pigouvian tax equal to the external cost) could internalise the externality and move output toward the social optimum.

IntroductionAO1 · AO3

Market failure occurs when the free market leads to a misallocation of resources — producing too much (negative externalities), too little (positive externalities, merit goods), or none at all (public goods). The question is whether government intervention is always required to correct these failures, or whether alternative mechanisms — including private bargaining and market-based solutions — can sometimes achieve efficient outcomes without state action.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)The definition mark. The three-way split is what lets the essay pick its cases later instead of arguing about one.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Reading "always" is the whole essay. An answer that argues intervention is useful has answered a question that was not asked.

Argument 1 — intervention is necessaryAO1 · AO2 · AO3

In many cases, government intervention is essential because the market cannot self-correct. Public goods such as street lighting and national defence are non-excludable and non-rivalrous — the free rider problem means no private firm can profitably supply them. Only the state can fund provision through taxation. The UK’s sugar tax (Soft Drinks Industry Levy), introduced in 2018, reduced sugary drink consumption by 34% and incentivised manufacturers to reformulate products. Without this intervention, the overconsumption of sugar — and its associated health-system costs — would have continued uncorrected.

Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)The claim the paragraph then has to earn. On its own it is worth nothing.
Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)Knowledge, and the strongest case for "always": with a pure public good there is no private route at all.
Earns 2 marks · AO2 — application (4 marks)Two application marks, because it is a named policy with a measured effect and a second-order effect. An example with no number earns one at most.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)The counterfactual. It is what turns an example into an argument, and most scripts leave it out.

Argument 2 — intervention is not always necessaryAO1 · AO2 · AO3

The Coase theorem suggests that if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are low, private parties can negotiate an efficient outcome without government involvement. For example, if a factory pollutes a farmer’s land and the farmer has clear legal ownership, they can negotiate compensation or a reduction in pollution — reaching the socially optimal output through bargaining. In practice, however, transaction costs are often high and property rights unclear, limiting the applicability of this approach.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)Knowledge, and the two conditions matter: quoting Coase without them makes the counter-argument unfalsifiable.
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (4 marks)Application to a case where the two conditions actually hold: two parties, one clear title.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)Analysis that turns the counter-argument into a bounded one. Without it the essay argues against itself in the conclusion.

Evaluation — government failureAO1 · AO3 · AO4

Even when intervention is attempted, it can lead to government failure — where the intervention makes the allocation of resources worse rather than better. Governments face information failure — they may not know the exact size of an external cost, leading them to set taxes too high or too low. Regulatory capture means regulators may serve industry interests rather than the public. Unintended consequences are common — for example, minimum pricing on alcohol may harm low-income moderate drinkers without significantly reducing problem drinking. The cost of intervention itself (bureaucracy, enforcement, compliance) may exceed the welfare gain from correcting the failure.

Earns 1 mark · AO1 — knowledge (4 marks)"Worse rather than better" is the definition mark. Government failure is not intervention that merely disappoints.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)The chain that connects back to the first argument: the same tax praised above can miss in either direction.
Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Evaluation, because it questions whose interest the intervention serves — a different objection from "it did not work".
Earns 1 mark · AO2 — application (4 marks)Application inside the evaluation, and it names who is harmed. A generic "there may be side effects" earns nothing.
Earns 1 mark · AO3 — analysis (6 marks)The comparison the conclusion will turn on, stated once here so the conclusion introduces no new material.

ConclusionAO4

On balance, government intervention is often necessary but not always effective or desirable. For pure public goods and large-scale externalities where private solutions are impractical, intervention is essential. However, the form and extent of intervention matters — well-designed market-based instruments (tradable permits, targeted taxes) tend to outperform heavy-handed regulation. The key judgement is whether the costs of government failure are likely to be greater or less than the costs of market failure left uncorrected. Where transaction costs are low and property rights clear, private solutions should be tried first.

Earns 2 marks · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Two marks: a conditional verdict that answers "always" with "not always, and here is when". A yes or a no cannot reach the top band on this question.
Earns 1 mark · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Judging the form, not just the fact. It is the move that separates a top-band conclusion from a summary.
Earns 2 marks · AO4 — evaluation (6 marks)Two marks. This is the evaluative framework itself — a criterion for deciding, not another opinion — and it closes the loop on both arguments.
